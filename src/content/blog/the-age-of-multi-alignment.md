For most of the twentieth century, foreign policy was a seating chart. You sat with one bloc or the other, and if you tried to sit in the middle you were told — politely or otherwise — that the middle was not a chair.

That chart has been torn up. A growing number of countries now practise what diplomats call **multi-alignment**: deep partnerships with rivals at the same time, chosen issue by issue rather than camp by camp. Buy energy here, defence technology there, sign a trade deal with one, join a summit with the other.

## Not fence-sitting, but bargaining

Critics call it fence-sitting. That misreads the game. Fence-sitters are passive; multi-aligners are active traders. Every partnership is a chip, and the value of each chip rises precisely because it is *not* exclusive.

> The more suitors you have, the less any one of them can dictate terms.

This is the logic a mid-sized economy uses when it negotiates with larger ones. Exclusivity is expensive. Optionality is leverage.

## Why now?

Three shifts made the middle a viable place to stand:

- **A diffusion of power.** No single capital can underwrite every country's security and prosperity at once.
- **Economic entanglement.** Supply chains crisscross rival camps; decoupling fully is costly for everyone.
- **Domestic politics.** Voters in large democracies reward leaders who look independent, not deferential.

## The risks of standing in the middle

Multi-alignment isn't free. It demands diplomatic bandwidth, a reputation for keeping promises to *everyone*, and the nerve to absorb pressure when great powers ask, loudly, "which side are you on?"

There's also a ceiling. In a genuine crisis, ambiguity can look like unreliability, and partners may hedge against *you*.

## A sangam, not a seating chart

The Indian image I keep returning to is the *sangam* — the confluence where rivers meet without any one of them ceasing to exist. Multi-alignment at its best is that: a country remaining itself while letting many currents pass through it.

Whether that holds in the decade ahead is the question this journal will keep circling back to.
